# sys

`/sys` is the kernel's structured view of the hardware, exposed as a virtual filesystem mounted at boot. Like [[proc]] it lives in memory, not on disk, and its files are generated by the kernel the moment you read them. The difference in role is real: `/proc` grew up around processes and kernel tunables, while `sysfs` was built later to present devices, drivers, and buses in a clean, hierarchical layout.

Walk into `/sys/class/net` and you see one directory per network interface; `/sys/block` lists your storage devices. Each leaf is usually a single attribute, so reading `/sys/class/power_supply/BAT0/capacity` gives you the battery percentage and nothing else. Many attributes are writable too, which is how userspace flips a setting like an LED's brightness or a CPU's frequency governor.

`udev` leans on `sysfs` to know what hardware exists and what events it fires, then creates the matching nodes you actually open in [[dev]]. So the split is clean: `/sys` describes the devices, `/dev` lets you talk to them.
